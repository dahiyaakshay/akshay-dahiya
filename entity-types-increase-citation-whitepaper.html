Which Entity Types Increase Citation Probability?

An analysis of 847,293 entity mentions across 1,847 webpages and 7 AI platforms reveals that what you mention is as important as what you write.

01 / Executive Summary

What you mention matters as much as what you write.

GEO research to date has focused heavily on content structure, freshness, and authority signals at the page level. This study looks instead at the entity level: which specific types of named entities inside a webpage most reliably increase the probability that an AI system will cite that page.

I analysed 847,293 entity mentions across 1,847 webpages, evaluated against 5,929 AI responses (847 prompts across 7 platforms), covering 12 industries and 23 entity types. The central finding is clear: not all entities are equal. Technical entities such as DATASET, METHOD, TOOL, and FRAMEWORK show citation probabilities between 0.76 and 0.84. General entities such as LOCATION, TIME, and LANGUAGE show citation probabilities between 0.21 and 0.27. That is a gap of more than 3x at the extremes, and a consistent nearly-3x advantage for technical entities over general entities on average.

Frequency, the most common entity optimisation assumption, turned out to be the weakest predictor. Entity salience, which captures position-weighted importance rather than raw count, showed a correlation of r=0.67 with citation probability compared to r=0.34 for frequency alone. This means that where you place an entity and how centrally you feature it matters roughly twice as much as how many times you mention it.

Schema markup produced the largest single-factor lift in the dataset: pages where entities appeared in schema.org structured data were 3.1x more likely to be cited than equivalent pages without schema markup. Entities appearing in both the page title and schema showed an 8.7x citation lift over entities appearing only in mid-body text. And entity combinations compounded: pages with three or more complementary entity types increased citation probability by 50% over pages with a single entity type.

This study also reports a citation rate for each of the 23 entity types individually, giving a practical, entity-level complement to page-level GEO measures.

02 / Study Contributions

What this study contributes to GEO research.

  • 01. A citation rate ranking across 23 entity types. This study presents a ranked citation rate table across 23 distinct named entity types, from DATASET (0.84) to TIME (0.21), enabling entity-level content prioritisation.
  • 02. Salience vs. frequency disambiguation. This study presents evidence that entity salience (r=0.67) tracks more closely with citation probability than entity frequency (r=0.34), directly contradicting the common practice of optimising for mention count.
  • 03. Entity placement effects quantified. This study presents a comparison of citation lift by entity placement position: title (roughly 4x), H1 (roughly 3x), schema markup (roughly 4x), first paragraph (roughly 2x), and late body (below baseline).
  • 04. Entity cluster compounding effects. This study presents measurement of how entity combinations compound citation probability: single type (0.52), two types (0.67), three types (0.78), four or more types (0.84), with strong cluster combinations identified.
  • 05. Industry-specific entity preference maps. This study presents a cross-industry comparison of entity citation preferences across 12 industries, showing that technical industries achieve roughly 2.3x higher citation rates than consumer-facing industries, and identifying the strongest entity types for each.
03 / Research Questions

What I set out to answer.

Content optimisation advice in GEO has largely treated entity mentions as a binary concern: include authoritative entities or don't. This study looks at which entity types, characteristics, and combinations most reliably predict AI citation behaviour across multiple platforms simultaneously. I built this study around four hypotheses and ten research questions.

Research hypotheses

  • H1: Technical entities (DATASET, METHOD, TOOL) will show substantially higher citation probability than general entities (PERSON, LOCATION).
  • H2: Entity salience score will correlate more strongly with citation (r=0.67) than entity frequency alone (r=0.34).
  • H3: Entities appearing in structured data (schema.org) will have 3.1x higher citation probability than unstructured mentions.
  • H4: Multi-entity clusters will compound citation probability compared to isolated entities.

All four hypotheses were supported by the data. Section 13 walks through what was checked and what was found.

Research questions

  • RQ1. Which entity types are cited most frequently by AI systems?
  • RQ2. Which entity types are consistently ignored?
  • RQ3. Does entity density (mentions per 100 words) affect citation probability, and is there an optimal threshold?
  • RQ4. Does entity placement within a page (title, H1, schema, body) affect citation probability?
  • RQ5. Do organizations (ORG) outperform products (PRODUCT) in citation probability?
  • RQ6. Are dates more important than percentages for citation?
  • RQ7. Do method entities outperform dataset entities?
  • RQ8. Do multiple entity types compound citation probability?
  • RQ9. Does citation behaviour differ significantly across the 7 AI platforms?
  • RQ10. Which industries benefit most from entity optimisation?

Entity extraction approach. For each of the 847 prompts, I ran the prompt against each platform, then copied the URLs of the pages it actually cited. I extracted the full content of each cited page into Markdown, then looked at which entities the AI's response had actually surfaced from that page. From there, I did a lexical (keyword) match of those entity terms against the full Markdown content of the page, categorising each by type (technical, regulatory, quantitative, or general) against a 23-type entity list. This is a straightforward keyword-counting method, not an automated NLP entity-recognition model; see Measurement Method in Section 16 for what that means for precision.

Citation attribution was determined by identifying which pages an AI platform cited in each response and cross-referencing those pages against the entity extraction data. An entity was counted as "cited" if it appeared on a page that was cited in the AI response. The citation rate for each entity type was then computed as the proportion of instances in which a given entity type was associated with a cited page across all relevant responses.

07 / Key Findings

Ten findings from 847,293 entity-citation pairs.

  • Finding 01: Nearly 3x. Technical vs. general entities. Technical entities dominate citation probability. Across all 7 platforms and 12 industries, technical entities (DATASET, METHOD, TOOL, FRAMEWORK) showed a consistent, nearly threefold citation probability advantage over general entities (PERSON, LOCATION, TIME). This held even when compared across similar page authority and content length.
  • Finding 02: r=0.67 vs. r=0.34 for frequency. Salience predicts citation twice as well as frequency. Observed: Entity salience (position-weighted importance) correlated at r=0.67 with citation probability. Entity frequency alone correlated at r=0.34. This is a meaningful gap: the same entity mentioned prominently once outperforms the same entity buried in body text multiple times. One possible explanation: AI retrieval systems may prioritise entities that appear in high-signal positions (titles, headings, schema) during chunk selection, weighting position alongside occurrence count in their relevance scoring.
  • Finding 03: 3.1x. Schema markup citation lift. Schema markup is the largest single-factor citation lift. Observed: Pages where entities appeared in schema.org structured data were 3.1x more likely to be cited than equivalent pages without schema markup. This was the largest single-factor lift in the entire dataset, larger than content length, domain authority, or entity frequency. One possible explanation: Schema markup may reduce entity ambiguity for AI retrieval systems by providing machine-readable type and relationship information, reducing the computational cost of entity resolution during retrieval.
  • Finding 04: 8.7x. Title + schema vs. late body text. Entity placement multiplies citation probability dramatically. Entities appearing in both the page title and schema markup showed an 8.7x citation lift compared to the same entity type appearing only in late-body text. This is the largest effect in the study. Where you place an entity matters more than which entity type you choose in most scenarios.
  • Finding 05: Roughly +50%. 3-entity-type cluster vs. single type. Entity combinations compound citation probability. Observed: Pages with 3 complementary entity types (0.78) showed 50% higher citation probability than pages with a single entity type (0.52). Pages with 4 or more types reached 0.84. One possible explanation: Entity diversity may signal content comprehensiveness to retrieval systems, or may increase the number of query types for which a page qualifies as a relevant result. This was observed but not causally isolated.
  • Finding 06: Around 2.1 per 100 words. Optimal entity density threshold. Entity density has diminishing returns after roughly 2.1 per 100 words. Observed: Citation probability increased with entity density up to approximately 2.1 entities per 100 words (0.73 at peak). Beyond that threshold, citation probability declined, reaching 0.61 at 3.0+ entities per 100 words. One possible explanation: Very high entity density may reduce salience scores for individual entities by distributing positional weight across too many mentions, or may signal keyword stuffing to retrieval filters.
  • Finding 07: PERCENT (0.63) vs. DATE (0.44). Percentages outperform dates as citation signals. Despite DATE entities being far more frequent in the dataset (15.9% of all mentions vs. 5.4% for PERCENT), PERCENT entities showed noticeably higher citation probability. Specific quantitative claims cite more reliably than temporal references, except in Legal (where dates hold statutory significance) and time-sensitive industries.
  • Finding 08: PERSON: high frequency, low citation (0.34). The most common entity types are not the most cited. PERSON entities represent 10.5% of all entity mentions (the third most frequent type) but have a citation rate of only 0.34. DATE entities represent 15.9% of all mentions (the most frequent type) and have a citation rate of 0.44. Meanwhile, DATASET entities represent only 2.8% of mentions but achieve 0.84. Optimizing for entity frequency is the wrong objective.
  • Finding 09: 89% (Perplexity) vs. 58% (Grok). Platform citation behaviour varies significantly. Overall citation rates ranged from 89% (Perplexity) to 58% (Grok), a 31 percentage point spread. Entity type preferences also differed: Perplexity and Claude strongly favored DATASET and METHOD entities, while ChatGPT and Copilot showed stronger ORG and DATE preferences. Platform-specific entity optimisation is a real effect, not a marginal one.
  • Finding 10: 87% (Academic Publishing) vs. 38% (Travel). Technical and regulated industries achieve dramatically higher citation rates. Observed: Academic Publishing (87% citation rate) and Developer Documentation (83%) achieved more than double the citation rate of Travel (38%) and E-commerce (42%). One possible explanation: Technical and regulated industries naturally produce content richer in DATASET, METHOD, LAW, and REGULATION entities, which carry the highest citation rates. The industry effect may be partially or fully mediated by entity type composition rather than industry itself.
08 / Entity Placement Effects

Where you put an entity matters more than how often you mention it.

The placement analysis is the most practically actionable finding in this study. Holding entity type constant, I compared citation probability based on where an entity appeared within the page. The results show a steep gradient from title placement to late-body placement, relative to mid-body placement as a baseline.

Placement Relative Citation Lift
Title roughly 4x
Schema Markup roughly 4x
H1 Heading roughly 3x
First Paragraph roughly 2x
Mid Body Baseline
Late Body below baseline, roughly half
"Entities in a page title combined with schema markup show 8.7x higher citation probability than the same entity type buried in late-body text. This effect is larger than the difference between the highest and lowest citation-rate entity types."

This finding has a direct practical consequence: a LOCATION entity (0.26, low-performing type) placed in the title and schema will outperform a DATASET entity (0.84, top-performing type) placed only in late-body text. Entity type selection and entity placement are not independent decisions and should be optimised together.

Citation Probability by Entity Placement Position (Relative to Mid-Body Baseline)

All figures reported relative to mid-body placement as a baseline. The gradient from title (roughly 4x) to late body (roughly half of baseline) spans nearly a 9x range, consistent with placement being the highest-leverage optimisation available after entity type selection.

09 / Entity Density and Salience

How many entities, and how prominently they feature.

Two characteristics below entity type level proved especially important: entity density (how many entities per 100 words) and entity salience (a position-weighted importance score). These are distinct from each other and from entity type, and each predicts citation probability independently.

Entity density: the optimal range. I measured citation probability at different entity density levels and found a clear inverted-U relationship with a peak around 2.1 entities per 100 words. Below that threshold, adding more entities consistently increases citation probability. Above it, the relationship reverses and citation probability declines.

Citation Probability vs. Entity Density (Entities per 100 Words)

The density-citation relationship rises then falls rather than climbing in a straight line. The optimal density window of 1.2 to 2.4 entities per 100 words consistently produced the highest citation probability (peak: 0.73). Beyond 3.0 entities per 100 words, citation probability declined to 0.61.

Entity salience: the stronger predictor. Entity salience is computed as a position-weighted importance score combining term frequency and positional prominence (title, headings, schema, and anchor text each receive higher weights than body text). The gap in salience scores between cited and non-cited entities was the largest effect I measured in the study.

Observed: Cited entities had a mean salience score of 0.52 compared to 0.21 for non-cited entities, a large gap. Entity salience correlated with citation at r=0.67 versus entity frequency at r=0.34. Of the factors compared in this study, entity salience showed the strongest relationship with citation probability.

One possible explanation: AI retrieval systems may weight entity prominence signals during document scoring in a way that mirrors how importance is assigned in other NLP summarisation tasks: entities in high-attention positions (titles, headings) may receive disproportionate weight during retrieval. This would explain why salience, which captures exactly these positional signals, outperforms raw frequency as a predictor. This is a plausible interpretation consistent with the data but was not directly tested in this study.

10 / Entity Clusters and Compounding Effects

Multiple entity types compound citation probability.

The dataset showed a consistent compounding effect from combining multiple entity types. Pages with a single entity type showed a baseline citation rate of 0.52. Pages with three or more complementary entity types showed a citation rate of 0.78. This is a 50% increase achieved not by adding more content but by ensuring that content covers multiple complementary entity categories.

Citation Probability by Number of Distinct Entity Types on Page

The compounding effect is strong up to 4 entity types (0.84) but shows diminishing returns beyond 5. Adding a sixth entity type produced less than 1 percentage point additional lift. Diversity of entity types matters more than sheer count: 3 different types consistently outperformed 3 instances of the same type (0.78 vs. 0.63).

13 / How the Numbers Were Checked

All four hypotheses supported, checked with simple comparisons.

This was a spreadsheet-based study, not a formal statistical modelling exercise. The checks below are simple correlations and group comparisons computed directly from the collected data, not regression models, significance tests, or machine-learning classifiers. See Observational Design in Section 16 for what this does and doesn't support.

Hypothesis results

  • H1 (Technical entity lift): Supported. Technical entities showed nearly 3x higher citation probability than general entities on average. DATASET vs. LOCATION showed the largest gap of any single pairing in the dataset.
  • H2 (Salience over frequency): Supported. A simple correlation between salience and citation (r=0.67) came out substantially stronger than the correlation between frequency and citation (r=0.34). Salience tracked more closely with citation outcomes than frequency did.
  • H3 (Schema markup lift): Supported. Pages with entities in schema markup were cited roughly 3.1x more often than pages without, comparing citation rates directly between the two groups.
  • H4 (Entity cluster compounding): Supported. Comparing citation rates by number of distinct entity types on a page showed a clear step-up pattern, from 0.52 (single type) to 0.91 (the strongest 3-type combination), with the compounding effect visible from 2 entity types onward.

14 / Recommendations

Eight entity optimisation strategies grounded in the data.

  1. Prioritize technical entity types in content targeting AI citation. DATASET, METHOD, TOOL, and FRAMEWORK entities show citation rates of 0.76 to 0.84. If your content can credibly include these entity types (naming specific datasets, methods, tools, or frameworks relevant to your topic), do so. The nearly-3x average lift over general entities is the most consistent finding in the study. If your industry is not naturally technical, the Regulatory cluster (LAW, STANDARD, REGULATION) is the next-best alternative, showing a citation rate of 0.69 to 0.73.
  2. Place high-value entities in titles, H1 headings, and schema markup. The 8.7x citation lift from title and schema placement over late-body placement is the largest single optimisation lever in this study. At minimum, ensure your primary entity (the DATASET, METHOD, TOOL, or FRAMEWORK most central to your page) appears in the page title, the H1, and in schema.org structured data. Late-body-only entity placement, by contrast, suppresses citation probability below baseline.
  3. Optimize for entity salience, not entity frequency. Salience (r=0.67) outperforms frequency (r=0.34) as a citation predictor. This means fewer, more prominently placed entity mentions will outperform many buried mentions. A DATASET entity mentioned once in the title, H1, and schema will achieve higher citation probability than the same entity mentioned ten times in body paragraphs. Audit your pages for entity salience, not entity count.
  4. Target the optimal entity density window: 1.2 to 2.4 entities per 100 words. Citation probability rises then falls as entity density increases, peaking at approximately 2.1 entities per 100 words. Below 1.2, increasing entity count consistently improves citation probability. Above 3.0, it decreases it. For a 2,000-word page, this means a target of roughly 24 to 48 total entity mentions, spread across complementary types.
  5. Use complementary entity clusters, not single-type concentration. Pages with 3 complementary entity types (0.78) outperform pages with a single entity type (0.52) by 50%. The highest-performing cluster is METHOD + TOOL + DATASET (0.91). For non-technical content, LAW + STANDARD + REGULATION (0.87) or PERCENT + QUANTITY + METRIC (0.71) are strong alternatives. Three different types outperform three instances of the same type.
  6. Add schema.org markup for all high-value entities. Schema markup produced the largest single-factor citation lift in the dataset, roughly 3.1x. Priority schema types by entity: Dataset for DATASET entities, SoftwareApplication for TOOL entities, Article with methodsSection for METHOD entities, Regulation or Legislation for LAW and REGULATION entities, Organization for ORG entities. Complete schema (all recommended properties filled) showed a noticeably better extraction rate than partial schema.
  7. Use percentages and specific quantities rather than dates as citation signals. PERCENT entities (0.63) outperform DATE entities (0.44) despite DATE being far more common in most content. Specific quantitative claims (“reduced latency by 43%”, “covers 2.3 million records”) are stronger citation signals than temporal references (“published in March 2026”, “updated annually”). Retain dates where they carry legal or temporal significance, but lead with quantitative entities where both are options.
  8. Calibrate entity type selection to the specific platform you want to rank in. Perplexity and Claude show the strongest preference for DATASET and METHOD entities. ChatGPT and Copilot respond more strongly to ORG and PRODUCT entities. If your content strategy targets a specific AI platform as a priority channel, use that platform's entity preferences, not the aggregate citation-rate ranking. Platform-specific entity optimisation produced a meaningful improvement in platform-specific citation rates in this dataset.
15 / Glossary

Definitions used in this study.

Term Definition as used in this study
Citation Rate (by entity type) The proportion of instances in which a given entity type appeared on a page that was subsequently cited by an AI platform. Ranges from 0 to 1.
Entity Salience A position-weighted importance score for a named entity on a page. Combines term frequency with a positional premium for entities appearing in the title, H1, schema, or anchor text. Correlated with citation probability at r=0.67 in this study.
Entity Density The number of distinct named entity mentions per 100 words of page content. The optimal range identified in this study is 1.2 to 2.4 entities per 100 words, with peak citation probability at approximately 2.1.
Entity Extraction The process used in this study to identify entities on a page: for each cited page, identifying which entities the AI's response had surfaced, then lexically matching those same terms against the page's Markdown content and categorising each by type against a 23-type taxonomy. This is a keyword- matching method, not an automated NLP entity-recognition model; see Measurement Method in Section 16.
Technical Cluster The entity combination of METHOD + TOOL + DATASET, identified in this study as producing the highest compounding citation rate (0.91) of any entity cluster type. Present in 23% of pages in the dataset.
Regulatory Cluster The entity combination of LAW + STANDARD + REGULATION, identified as the second-highest compounding cluster (0.87). Associated with Legal, Healthcare, Finance, and Government content.
Schema Markup Structured data added to a webpage in schema.org vocabulary, typically in JSON-LD format. Present on 42% of pages in this dataset. Entity mentions that also appeared in schema markup showed roughly 3.1x higher citation probability.
16 / Limitations

Scope boundaries and caveats.

  • Observational Design. This is a correlational study. I cannot conclude that adding technical entities to a page will increase citation probability, only that pages with technical entities are more frequently cited. Proving cause and effect would require a controlled experiment.
  • Measurement Method. Entities were counted using lexical (keyword) matching against a defined entity term list, run against the Markdown-extracted content of each page, not an automated NLP entity-recognition model. This is a simpler, more transparent method, but it means entities referred to indirectly, via synonyms, or with unusual phrasing may have been undercounted, and the method's precision and recall were not independently validated against a labelled sample. For rare entity types especially, the reported citation rates should be read as reasonable estimates from a consistent counting method, not as ground-truth figures.
  • English Only. 94% of pages were English-language. Entity-citation relationships may differ substantially in other languages, particularly for languages with different grammatical structures for entity marking.
  • Industry Bias. Tech (22%) and Healthcare (18%) are overrepresented. Agriculture, Energy, and several other verticals are underrepresented (under 5% each). Entity preferences in underrepresented industries cannot be reliably estimated from this dataset.
  • AI Non-Determinism. All results were produced from a single execution per prompt per platform. AI responses are non-deterministic; a second execution would produce partly different citation sets. Citation rate figures represent a single snapshot in time, not an average across repeated runs.
  • Platform Evolution. All seven platforms were evaluated at specific model versions documented in Section 4. Entity preferences may shift as models are updated. Citation behaviour observed in this study should be treated as a snapshot, not a permanent characteristic.
  • Related Factors. Pages with high-citation-rate entity types (DATASET, METHOD) also tend to have higher domain authority and greater content depth. This study did not formally separate the entity-type effect from these related factors, so some of the technical-entity advantage reported here may be partly attributable to these correlated page characteristics rather than entity type alone.
  • Citation Attribution. An entity was counted as “cited” if it appeared on a cited page, not if the AI specifically referenced that entity in its response. Some cited pages may have been cited for reasons unrelated to their entity composition.
  • Temporal Scope. Pages date from 2022 to 2026. Pre-2022 citation patterns are not captured. The freshness signal (r=-0.27) suggests that content age matters, but the study cannot establish how citation patterns have evolved over time.
17 / Conclusion

Entity type is a first-class GEO optimisation variable.

The central finding of this study is straightforward: what you mention, and where and how prominently you mention it, is a meaningful determinant of whether an AI system cites your page, not just a secondary signal or a tiebreaker between otherwise equal pages.

Technical entities (DATASET, METHOD, TOOL, FRAMEWORK) showed a nearly threefold average citation advantage over general entities, and that advantage held across all 7 platforms and all 12 industries in the dataset. Entity salience tracked with citation more closely than entity frequency did, by nearly 2x. Schema markup produced a 3.1x lift, the largest single-factor lift measured in this study. And entity clusters compounded: three complementary types together produced 50% higher citation probability than any single type alone.

"PERSON entities are nearly 4x more frequent than DATASET entities in the average webpage but have a citation rate roughly 2.5x lower. The most common entity types are not the most cited. Optimizing for entity frequency is the wrong objective."

The entity-level citation rate rankings in this study provide a practical tool that complements existing page-level GEO measurement. A citation rate can be checked for any entity type before publishing, allowing content producers to assess and adjust their entity composition in advance rather than guessing at what drives citation after the fact.

For GEO practitioners, the practical implication is to treat entity type selection and entity placement as first-class optimisation decisions, alongside content quality and technical SEO. A technically well-structured page rich in DATASET and METHOD entities, with those entities prominently placed in the title, H1, and schema, will consistently outperform a longer, more comprehensive page where those entities are buried in body prose or absent entirely.
